Firm-State
JEPA

I wanted to know whether a company's financial history carries information its current balance sheet does not. This is the full record of finding out ; the question, every decision, what broke, and what the data said back.

Period 2013Q1 – 2020Q4 Firms 11,742 Observations 215,944 Result 1 finding, 4 negatives

Entry 00 ; the one-sentence version

Companies are trajectories, not documents

Every AI system built for accounting and legal work treats a client as a pile of text. Chunk the documents, embed the chunks, retrieve the relevant ones. That works for finding things. It does not work for noticing that something is wrong.

A client firm is not a pile. It is a state that evolves. A ledger in March is February's ledger plus a set of transactions. That is a transition model, not a retrieval problem. This project tests whether treating it that way actually buys anything measurable.

The claim under test If firm financial state is genuinely path-dependent, a model trained on correctly-ordered histories should predict the future better than the same model trained on shuffled ones. If order doesn't matter, the whole framing is wrong and the honest move is to say so.

The answer, before the details

Nothing below is a build-up to a product. One thing worked, several did not, and the failures took more effort to establish than the success. Stated at the top so no one has to scroll for it:

Order carries predictive signalYES · +21.4%, monotone in length
JEPA collapses onto firm sizeYES · mechanism diagnosed, entry 07
Early warning of firm exitNO · flat at every horizon
Early warning of restatementNO · flat at every horizon
Detection at arrivalWEAK · d = 0.24 vs 0.13–0.16 floor
Label-efficient probingNO · 17–30% of own lift at N=50

Two of those negatives were originally positives that did not survive being checked properly ; one an unfair evaluation, one an event set that was 41.9% artifact. Those checks are entries 08 and 11, and they are the parts of this worth reading.

Entry 01 ; the idea that started it

Why I thought this might work

This section is the hypothesis, not the result. Each part of it carries the verdict the experiments returned, stated here rather than four entries later, because the shape of the reasoning is worth keeping even where the data disagreed with it.

The observation the whole thing rests on

Suppose an accounting firm reconciles a hundred client ledgers a month and wants to know which ones to look at. Every available tool needs labels. Beneish and Dechow were fitted on known fraud cases. Distress models need bankruptcies. Rules engines need somebody to write rules, one at a time, forever.

That firm has zero labeled anomalies. Nobody kept that dataset, and the clients are private, so there are no restatements or enforcement filings to borrow. Every supervised method is unusable on day one and stays that way until someone spends two years labeling.

A model that trains on each client's own history predicting its own next state needs none of that. Prediction error becomes a signal at zero labeling cost. That asymmetry is what made the idea seem worth a few weeks.

Three things I expected to fall out, and what happened to each

Client-specific anomaly detection · did not hold The reasoning: a population-trained detector flags deviation from a global norm, which is why such systems fire constantly on seasonal or structurally unusual firms and get muted. A per-client transition model would learn what normal means for that client. Measured (Entry 11): no early warning of anything, at any horizon, for either event type, under every scoring variant. Detection at arrival is weak ; d = 0.24 against a pre-event noise band of 0.13 to 0.16.
Confidence routing · untested The reasoning: extraction pipelines label outputs verified, extracted or estimated using heuristics about the source. Prediction error is a better basis ; if the extracted number matches what the state model expects, skip the human. Status: never built. It inherits whatever the anomaly signal is worth, and Entry 11 says that is currently not much.
Label efficiency · did not hold The reasoning: a frozen encoder should let a firm with fifty labeled examples fit a linear probe, where training from scratch would need thousands. Measured (Entry 10): at fifty labels the encoder recovers 17–30% of its own full-label performance, and never beats gradient boosting at equal label count.

What did survive is narrower and sits underneath all three: the claim that a firm's history carries information its current state does not. That is Entry 09, it is the one thing the project established, and it is a statement about representation rather than about products.

The interesting question was never "does this beat fraud detection." It was whether order in a company's financial history means anything at all. That one has an answer.

Entry 02 ; the data

Why SEC filings, when the target is private firms

Private client ledgers are not obtainable, and never will be for a project like this. But the mechanism has to be proved somewhere, and public company filings are the one place where firm-state trajectories are public and where restatements provide ground truth.

The source is the SEC Financial Statement Data Sets: free bulk downloads, standardized XBRL tags, every filer since 2009. Two files matter. sub.txt is one row per filing. num.txt is one row per reported number.

The academic literature on this data runs cross-sectional classifiers over hand-engineered ratios. Nobody has learned a latent transition model over firm state with a non-generative objective. So the data is well-trodden and the treatment is not.

Scope of the download: 32 quarterly ZIPs covering 2013Q1 through 2020Q4. 2.7 GB compressed, 11 GB extracted, roughly 100 million raw numeric rows.

Entry 03 ; four wrong assumptions

What the data turned out to actually be

The project spec described the data before anyone looked at it. Four of those descriptions were wrong, and each was wrong in the direction of underestimating the work. This is the part that took the longest and it is the part that would have quietly ruined everything downstream.

Wrong 01 ; a quarterly file is not a quarter of data The SEC bundles by filing date, not by period covered. So 2015q1.zip is overwhelmingly FY2014 annual reports, because calendar Q1 is 10-K season: 792,898 rows describing 2014 against 37,675 describing 2015. Worse, a single filing carries many periods ; median 5, maximum 44 ; because annual reports restate prior years as comparatives. Fix: an observation is (cik, ddate), assembled across many files. Never read off one.
Wrong 02 ; 46% of the rows are not firm-level A column the spec never mentioned, segments, marks rows that are breakdowns by geography, product line, or business unit. Another 10% are subsidiary-level via coreg. Without filtering these out, one tag has fifteen rows for one firm-period and the vector is undefined. Fix: consolidated rows only.
Wrong 03 ; flows are cumulative, and Q4 doesn't exist The qtrs column gives the duration a number covers. Balance-sheet items are instants and are clean. Income and cash-flow items are year-to-date: a Q3 filing reports nine months, not the third quarter. Only 15.7% of flow facts are discrete single quarters. And no filer reports Q4 ; the 10-K gives the full year, so Q4 must be derived by subtraction. Fix: Phase 1 uses balance-sheet instants only. Flows are deferred to Phase 2.
Wrong 04 ; trajectories are 18 quarters, not 40 to 60 Measured across the full 32-quarter window: median 18 balance-sheet quarters, p95 of 33, and a hard ceiling of 36 because that is the window. Only 0.52% of firms reach 36. The spec's 40–60 figure is not just wrong, it is untestable without data back to roughly 2005.

The one decision that mattered most: which version of a number to use

4.5% of facts get re-reported later with a different value ; and across the full 32 quarters, 51.4% of all facts were re-reports of a period already covered. So a vintage has to be chosen, and it is not a coin flip.

Decision ; as-first-reported, always Take the earliest filing date. Using latest-reported would give the corrected numbers, which means the original errors have been erased from the dataset. An anomaly detector would then be trained on data where the anomalies were quietly fixed, and evaluated on its ability to find them. It would look mysteriously bad and nobody would know why. This is lookahead leakage in its purest form.

Entry 04 ; the unit

Defining what counts as one observation

With 57,447 distinct tags in the data, something has to be chosen. Three facts shaped that choice.

91% of the tag vocabulary is filer-invented and covers only 7.5% of rows. Those custom tags don't align across firms ; they are private labels. Dropped.

Coverage falls off a cliff. Restricted to balance-sheet instants, not a single tag is reported by 90% of firm-quarters. This kills any dense-vector design and validates the decision to carry an explicit mask channel rather than filling absent values with zero. Which tags a firm reports is itself information about what kind of firm it is.

The same concept hides under different tags. Revenue is the worst case: Revenues, SalesRevenueNet, SalesRevenueGoodsNet and others fragment one economic idea, which is why revenue doesn't appear in the top 20 despite every firm having some. The fix is a hand-written concept layer mapping 100 member tags onto 34 canonical fields, with a documented priority order for firms reporting more than one.

A finding that cut against expectation: the balance-sheet core is not fragmented. Nine concepts ; total assets, total liabilities, retained earnings and others ; gained exactly zero from consolidation because they are single-tag concepts. Fragmentation lives in the mid-tier: paid-in capital, payables, receivables, debt splits. The catastrophic revenue-style fragmentation is an income-statement phenomenon, and therefore a Phase 2 problem.

One more definitional call. Nearly 42% of (cik, ddate) pairs carried three tags or fewer ; mostly a single cash figure lifted out of a cash-flow statement, which reaches back several periods and creates ghost dates where no balance sheet was ever filed.

Decision ; an observation IS a balance sheet It must report total assets or total liabilities-and-equity. This is a definition of the unit, not a density threshold. 80.4% of firm-quarters qualify.

Entry 05 ; normalization

Removing size, because size is not dynamics

Raw balance-sheet values span up to 18 orders of magnitude. Worse, 81.2% of the variance is between firms rather than within them. Apple is large this quarter and large next quarter.

That is a trap, not a nuisance. A model trained on raw values would predict future state well by encoding "this is a big industrial firm" and predicting it stays big. The prediction task becomes trivial, the representation becomes a size detector, and the order ablation returns a null ; not because order is irrelevant, but because the model never learned anything order could affect. A false negative on the project's central test is the most expensive error available.

Two of the highest-variance slots are majority-negative: aoci 67.8%, retained_earnings 57.6%. The left form silently mangles both.

Three further corrections were needed to make it work:

  • Two slots become different features. After ratioing, total assets is exactly 1.0 and liabilities-and-equity is 1.0 ± 1e-6 by the accounting identity. Z-scoring a slot whose variance is float noise amplifies that noise to unit variance and feeds the encoder garbage. Assets becomes the size dimension; liabilities-and-equity becomes a balance-residual indicator, which is a genuine anomaly feature.
  • A fallback cascade for firms with no size. 2,393 shell-company steps have zero assets. Falling back to liabilities-and-equity fails there too ; by the identity, if assets are zero, so is that. So: assets, else liabilities-and-equity, else the firm's own expanding-window median, else a global floor, with an indicator flag whenever the scale is inferred rather than observed. No rows dropped, because dropping them would be a completeness filter and those are forbidden.
  • Shares handled separately. Share count correlates with firm size at just 0.161 ; it reflects split history, not size.

Entry 06 ; architecture

The model

This is a JEPA ; a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture. The defining property is that it predicts in latent space, not in the space of the data. There is no decoder and no reconstruction. It never tries to output a balance sheet; it tries to predict its own future internal representation.

That matters here because reconstructing a balance sheet would force the model to spend capacity on exact dollar amounts, most of which are unpredictable noise. Predicting a latent lets it keep only what is structurally predictable.

(5) The four maps et = Eθ([ xt ⊙ mt ; mt ]) ∈ ℝ128 ht = Cθ(e1:t + Pos(τ1:t)) ẑt+k = Pθ(ht, k),   k = τt+k − τt ∈ [1, 8] zu = sg[Eξ(xu, mu)],   ξ ← λξ + (1−λ)θ
x 31 values · m 31 mask bits, concatenated to a 62-d input so the mask is a channel, not a filter
τ quarters since the firm's first observation, so a gap shifts position rather than looking contiguous
k is measured in quarters via τ, not in observation offsets ; the two differ exactly when a firm has a hole
sg stop-gradient · ξ the EMA target encoder, λ annealed 0.996 → 0.9999, kept in eval mode always
(6) Objective · prediction in latent space, plus anti-collapse ℒpred = (1/|P|d) Σ(t,u)∈P Σj SmoothL1(ẑuj − zuj) ℒ = ℒpred + α·v(E) + β·c(E),   α = 1.0, β = 0.04
P the set of valid (context, target) pairs · d = 128. No decoder appears anywhere: both sides of the loss are internal representations.

Entry 07 ; failure, and what it taught

Run 1 collapsed onto the size axis

The first training run looked excellent. Loss fell 17× in 500 steps. It was completely broken.

The instrumentation that caught it was Participation Ratio divided by latent dimension ; a measure of how many dimensions are actually carrying variance. It had been logged from step zero specifically because this failure mode is invisible in the loss curve.

(7) Participation ratio · effective dimensionality PR(Z) = (Σi λi)2  /  Σi λi2,   λ = eig(Cov(Z))
PR = d when every eigenvalue is equal ; variance spread evenly over all directions.
PR → 1 when one eigenvalue dominates ; all variance on a single axis.
Reported as PR / d so it reads as a fraction of the latent actually in use. There is no target value; the trend is the signal, and a sustained decline while loss improves is the stop-and-diagnose trigger.

The diagnosis refuted the obvious hypothesis

The expected failure was constant collapse ; all latents converging to a single point. The measurements said otherwise:

Variance grew, by more than a thousand times. The latents were not one point ; they were spread along one line. This is anisotropic collapse, and the mechanism is specific to this data:

The most predictable thing about a firm's next state is how big the firm is. Size barely moves within a firm. So the model learned to encode the size axis and discarded everything else.

Note that the normalization scheme had already been designed to fight exactly this, and the model found its way there anyway through a different route. Target LayerNorm ; the standard I-JEPA anti-collapse device ; cannot help, and the reason is visible in the definition:

(8) Why per-sample normalization cannot stop this LN(z)j = (zj − μ(z)) / σ(z),   μ, σ taken across j, per sample
The statistics are computed within one sample, across features. Every sample is normalized independently, so a direction shared by all samples survives untouched. LayerNorm constrains each latent to a sphere; it says nothing about where on that sphere the population sits. Measured: mean pairwise cosine of layer-normed targets was 0.58, not 1.0 ; not one point, one axis.
Fix ; variance regularization, and exactly one normalization active Target LayerNorm off. A VICReg-style variance hinge that forces every dimension to carry at least unit standard deviation, plus a covariance penalty that pushes dimensions to decorrelate. Only one normalization mechanism runs at a time, asserted in code, because a known failure mode is these two fighting each other.
(9) The two regularizers, computed across the batch v(E) = (1/d) Σj max(0, γ − √ Var(E·j) + ε ),   γ = 1 c(E) = (1/d) Σi≠j [Cov(E)]ij2
This is the whole fix. Unlike (8), Var and Cov are taken down the batch for each dimension, not across features within a sample ; which is precisely what makes them able to see, and penalize, a shared dominant axis.
v forces every one of the 128 dimensions to carry spread. c stops those dimensions from being copies of one another. Result: effective dimensions 1.3 → ~30.

Result: effective dimensions went from 1.3 to roughly 30, and stayed there. This finding ; that JEPA applied to data with a dominant static axis collapses onto that axis, and that per-sample normalization cannot prevent it ; generalizes well beyond financial data, and is arguably the most transferable thing the project produced.

Entry 08 ; a false negative

The result that was wrong, and how it got caught

With training stable, the decisive experiment ran: train one model on correctly-ordered histories, one on shuffled histories, compare. The first run came back negative. Shuffling didn't hurt ; on the regime-stable control window, shuffling helped by 10.91%.

The tell was not in the code. It was in the sign.

The diagnostic "Order is irrelevant" predicts a gap near zero. It does not predict the shuffled model beating the ordered one by 11%. A null that overshoots into the wrong sign is a broken comparison, not a strong finding.

Two bugs were stacked underneath it.

The evaluation was unfair. The shuffled arm was being scored on shuffled validation pairs, so the two arms were not doing the same task. In an ordered trajectory, predicting t+k means extrapolating drift, and the target moves further away as k grows. Shuffle the sequence and targets become near-exchangeable draws clustered around the firm's own mean. Shuffling makes the prediction problem intrinsically easier, entirely independently of whether order carries information.

The inputs were damaged. An earlier decision to clip extreme values at ±10 after standardization sounded harmless ; 70 entries out of 4.1 million. In practice it destroyed 95.5% of one slot's variance and 15–67% across a dozen others, including aoci, one of only two slots carrying most of its variance within firms. Within-firm variance is precisely where path dependence lives. The clip had attenuated the signal the experiment existed to detect.

Both fixed Both arms now evaluate on identical ordered validation pairs, with an assertion enforcing it. Clipping replaced by winsorizing in raw space at per-slot quantiles, then a single standardization pass, which converges where the previous approach did not.

But the headline number is not the evidence. The gradient across trajectory lengths is the evidence.

Caveats recorded alongside the result

  • The regime-stable control window is only 16 quarters wide, so the two longest buckets are empty by construction. The control confirms the effect at 16–23 but cannot reach where the largest gaps live. No 32-quarter regime-stable window exists in this data.
  • 11 of 30 slots still retain under 50% of their raw variance after winsorizing. This attenuates signal rather than manufacturing it, so the measured gap is conservative ; but the inputs are degraded.
  • One slot, common_shares_outstanding, is dead: the ratio is exactly 1.0 through the 99.5th percentile. No threshold rescues it.

A second finding, from a side experiment

Effective dimensionality lands at 29–34 regardless of latent width, but halving the width costs 62% in prediction error. So the extra width is not padding ; it is doing something other than adding dimensions, and roughly 30 dimensions appears to be the intrinsic dimensionality of firm balance-sheet state under this objective.

Entry 10 ; the probes failed, and the probes were wrong

Testing whether the latent means anything

Low prediction error only proves the model is self-consistent. It says nothing about whether those 128 numbers contain anything a person would recognise. A probe answers that: freeze the encoder, run the data through, then fit the simplest possible model ; logistic regression ; from latent to some property whose answer is already known.

The probe has to be linear and tiny. Allow a deep network on top and it could compute the answer itself, teaching you nothing about the latent. Keeping it linear means a good score can only come from the encoder having already arranged that information readably. Two baselines make the score interpretable: gradient boosting on the raw 31 slots asks whether the encoder was needed at all, and a mask-only run ; values zeroed, only the 31 present/absent bits ; asks whether the probe is reading which forms the firm filed rather than what the numbers say.

XGBoost wins all four. Two of the four fail the mask-only control, meaning they are largely reading taxonomy rather than state ; the distress probe most starkly, where a firm winding down simply files fewer tags, so "which fields are missing" beats anything about the values.

And the label-efficiency claim, which was the closest thing in the project to a direct deployment argument, does not hold. At 50 labels the frozen encoder recovers 17–30% of its own full-label performance, and never beats XGBoost at equal label count.

But the suite was mis-specified, and that is the more important finding Every label used ; sector, leverage ratio, ΔROA sign, distress-by-filing-cessation ; was computable from the current step alone. So the probes asked whether a 128-dimensional latent is a lossless compression of a 31-slot vector. It is not, and was never trained to be. The leverage probe is the reductio: its label is literally (debt_current + debt_noncurrent) / assets_total, three input slots in closed form. A tree reading the raw vector reconstructs that by arithmetic. No probe in the suite tested history, which is the encoder's entire claim. These results are weak evidence about representation quality in either direction.

Entry 11 ; surprise as a detector

Does the model notice when a firm departs from its own path?

This is the use case from Entry 01, tested. Compute per-quarter prediction error for every firm in the held-out set. Align each event firm's error series on the quarter something happened. Compare against matched controls ; same sector, same size band. If surprise rises before or at the event, the mechanism works.

One methodological decision shaped everything: only firms present at all nine offsets are used. The raw event count grew from 186 to 354 across offsets, and a rise measured over a changing firm set is composition, not signal. That balanced panel is what stopped a spurious ramp from being reported.

(11) Surprise · three scorings and the effect size sit = (1/d) Σj SmoothL1(ẑitj − zitj) causal z:   (sit − meds<t) / (1.4826 · MADs<t) sector-relative:   (sit − μg(i)) / σg(i),   g(i) = (sector, size band) dCohen = (x̄event − x̄control) / √ (σe2 + σc2)/2 
One-step-ahead error at the true quarter gap k = τt − τt−1, identical to the training objective ; a rise means the firm became less predictable from its own past.
d on the first line is the latent width (128); dCohen on the last is the effect size. Same letter, different quantities.
The causal z uses an expanding window over s < t only (≥4 priors), so nothing from the event period leaks backwards. Sector-relative is the default: it halved finance in the top-50 (27 → 12) and raised the effect.

Restatements ; a spike at arrival, and then it shrank

The first version looked strong: a flat run-up through t−1 and a jump to d = 0.44 at the restated quarter. Not anticipation, but a real unsupervised detector firing on the anomalous filing as it lands.

Then the event set itself was audited, and 41.9% of it turned out not to be restatements at all. The proxy's own algebra says why:

(12) The divergence proxy, and the artifact it hides ρit = maxtag  |vlast − vfirst| / max(|vfirst|, |vlast|) vfirst = 0, vlast ≠ 0  ⇒  ρ = 1  exactly sgn vfirst ≠ sgn vlast  ⇒  ρ = (|a|+|b|)/max(|a|,|b|) ∈ (1, 2]
So ρ = 1 is not a 100% move in a reported number ; it is a zero crossing. And ρ > 1 is only reachable by a sign flip. The naive event set's p75 sits at exactly 1.000 and its p90 at 1.552, which is the artifact signature stated in one line.
Selecting each firm's maximum-divergence quarter concentrates precisely this tail, which is why the firm-level artifact rate (41.9%) far exceeds the tag-level rate (13.1%).
Plausible = same-sign and ratio ≤ 10×, which drops both failure modes and the merger-holdco stubs.
What was actually in the event set The dominant artifact is the merger holdco registration stub: a newly formed entity files a nominal or zero balance sheet, and a later filing carries the real consolidated figures. Ares Management shows total assets of $1,000 first-reported against $23.7 billion later. Baker Hughes GE, Walgreens Boots Alliance, Linde, TechnipFMC, Broadcom Cayman ; all new holdcos formed by merger, nothing restated. Add sign flips (25.4% of selected events), zero-crossings, and scope changes above 10×, and 168 of 401 firms were implausible as restatements. Because events are selected by taking each firm's maximum-divergence quarter, the selection actively concentrates the artifact tail: the firm-level artifact rate of 41.9% far exceeds the tag-level rate of 13.1%.

The honest statement Direction survives, magnitude does not. Restatement detection at arrival is weak and marginal, and there is no early warning at any horizon for anything. Any phrasing that implies otherwise is overstating the evidence.

What the ranking was actually detecting

Reading the top-50 highest-surprise firm-quarters under sector-relative scoring, a pattern appears that nobody was looking for. Momentive Performance Materials and MPM Holdings at ranks 1 and 2 ; same corporate group, same quarter, both subsequently gone. TechnipFMC at rank 3, the merger quarter. Then Shopify 2015, Uber 2019, nCino 2020. Spinoffs: VICI, Parkway. SPACs: Allegro, Double Eagle, Hydra. WestRock, Eastman Kodak, General Steel.

Most of these carry no restatement signal at all. They are IPOs, mergers, spinoffs and SPACs ; genuine discontinuities in firm state, which a latent transition model arguably should flag. The balance sheet of a company before and after an IPO is a different object.

This is an observation from reading fifty names, not a measurement. It is exactly how spurious findings are born. It is testable ; S-1 effective dates, 8-K Item 2.01 completions, Form 10 spinoffs ; with the same timeline machinery already built, and it remains untested. It is listed here as a hypothesis the data suggested, not a result.

Entry 12 ; the ledger

What this project has, and what it does not

Stated plainly, because a writeup that blurs this line is worth nothing.

Holds up

  • Path dependence in firm financial state. Ordered training beats shuffled by 21.4% on the full window and 11.6% on a regime-stable control, with a monotone length gradient across six buckets on two disjoint windows and the shortest bucket at zero as a sanity check. Both arms healthy, neither collapsed. This is the finding.
  • The anisotropic collapse mechanism. JEPA on data with a dominant static axis collapses onto that axis while total variance grows, and per-sample normalisation cannot prevent it. Diagnosed with measurements, fixed with variance regularisation. This transfers well beyond financial data and is arguably the most portable thing here.
  • Intrinsic dimensionality of ~30 in firm balance-sheet state under this objective, stable across latent widths, with halving the width costing 62% in prediction error.
  • Two methodological results. A null that overshoots into the wrong sign is a broken comparison, not a strong finding ; that instinct caught a false negative produced by two stacked bugs. And an event-set audit that found 41.9% artifact in a proxy most people would have shipped unexamined.
  • The data work. Four spec assumptions about SEC filings were wrong; as-first-reported vintage selection turned out load-bearing at 51.4% re-reports.

Does not hold, or was never established

  • No early warning of anything. Flat at every horizon, both event types, every scoring variant.
  • Restatement detection is weak. d = 0.24 against a pre-event band of 0.13–0.16.
  • The probe suite established nothing in either direction, because its labels did not require history.
  • Label efficiency does not hold. 17–30% of the encoder's own lift at 50 labels.
  • The encoder-beats-naive-baseline result is unconfirmed. The 4× margin over a model-free per-slot z-score was measured on the contaminated 401-firm event set and was never rerun on the cleaned subset. It should not be cited until it is.
  • No action conditioning, so nothing can be rolled forward or planned in. A simulator predicts state conditional on actions, and filings contain no actions ; only the state that resulted from them. What this learns is p(zt+k | z1:t), an autonomous transition, not p(zt+1 | zt, at). It is a self-supervised sequence representation learner with a latent predictive objective.
  • Not hierarchical. Single-level masking only; the three-level variant was specified and never run.
  • No flows. Balance-sheet instants only. Income and cash-flow items, where the genuine per-period dynamics live, are untouched.

Entry 13 ; the deliverable

What comes out at the end

Not a product, and not a detector. One result, a mechanism, and an unusually complete account of what does not work.

  • A trained encoder and a full pipeline. Maps a firm's financial history to a ~30-dimensional latent state, self-supervised, no labels. It will not transfer to a private firm's ledgers ; different chart of accounts, no XBRL, different scale. What transfers is the method: architecture, collapse fix, normalisation scheme, ablation protocol. Anyone deploying this retrains from scratch on the target firm's own history.
  • A public repository and report in which the negatives occupy as much space as the positive, because they are most of what was learned.
  • One defensible claim with a number. Firm financial state is path-dependent: order carries 21% of predictive signal on the full window, 12% on a regime-stable control, monotone across length. That is the technical form of the argument that a client is a state, not a corpus.
  • One mechanism worth reusing. How and why JEPA collapses on data with dominant static structure, and what actually prevents it.

The original ambition was an unsupervised anomaly detector that needs no labels ; which matters because a firm reconciling a hundred client ledgers has none. Four separate experiments say that does not work here, each in a different way. That is an answered question rather than a failed project, but it is not the answer that was wanted, and the writeup should not pretend otherwise.

A clean negative on a falsifiable claim is a finding. A positive on a broken test is not. This project produced both kinds and learned to tell them apart.
