Smoothing Filters in the Frequency Domain

Sections 1–2 built the machinery (Fourier transform, DFT, sampling). This is the first section that actually uses it to do something to an image: remove high frequencies on purpose, to blur or denoise. Every idea here reduces to one formula, three different shapes for one filter, and one unavoidable trade-off.

3.1 · Where this sits in the bigger picture

Image enhancement splits into spatial-domain methods (Unit 1's territory: point processing like negatives and contrast stretching, and neighborhood processing like averaging/median filters) and frequency-domain methods, which is everything from here through Section 5. Deck 3's own map of the frequency-domain branch is worth keeping in view for the next three sections, since it previews exactly where each upcoming topic sits:

One more orienting fact worth pinning down before diving in — the frequency domain and the spatial domain describe the exact same image, just in two different coordinate systems:

3.2 · The one formula behind every filter in Sections 3–5

G(u,v) = H(u,v) · F(u,v)

F(u,v) is the DFT of the input image (Section 1.5). H(u,v) is the filter transfer function — a grid the same size as F(u,v), holding one multiplier per frequency. G(u,v) is the filtered result, still in the frequency domain; running the inverse DFT on G(u,v) gives back the filtered image. Every filter from here to the end of Section 5 is nothing more than a different recipe for H(u,v) — the multiplication step never changes, only the shape of H.

For smoothing, the recipe is simple: H(u,v) should be large (close to 1) near the center (the low frequencies) and small (close to 0) far from the center (the high frequencies), so that low frequencies pass through mostly untouched while high frequencies get suppressed. This is exactly why the technique is called a low-pass filter (LPF).

3.3 · The Ideal Low-Pass Filter

H(u,v) = 1  if D(u,v) ≤ D₀     H(u,v) = 0  if D(u,v) > D₀
where D(u,v) = [(u − M/2)² + (v − N/2)²]1/2

D(u,v) is just the Euclidean distance of a frequency coordinate (u,v) from the spectrum's center (the DC term, at (M/2, N/2) after the shift used for display). The "ideal" filter is the most literal possible reading of "drop everything past a cutoff": a hard disc of radius D₀ that passes every frequency inside it unchanged and kills every frequency outside it completely. It is called "ideal" because it is the mathematically simplest cutoff you could write down — not because it produces the best-looking result, as the next figure demonstrates.

Choosing D₀ by how much energy it keeps

Rather than picking D₀ arbitrarily, it can be chosen by asking "what radius circle, centered at the origin, enclosed α percent of the image's total spectral power?" — where power at (u,v) is |F(u,v)|² and the total power PT is the sum over the whole spectrum:

α = 100 · [ ΣΣ P(u,v) / PT ]

The slide's own test image shows the trade-off starkly: a tiny radius-5 disc already keeps 92% of the power (removes only 8%) yet the result is a smear with barely recognizable shapes. A radius-80 disc keeps 98% of the power and looks nearly indistinguishable from the original. Two things to notice: first, most of an image's energy really does sit at low frequency (echoing the energy-compaction preview from Section 1.5); second, even removing a "small" percentage of power at the wrong frequencies (namely, all the high ones) can be visually devastating, because energy and visual importance are not the same currency — a small amount of high-frequency energy carries a disproportionate amount of edge and detail information.

Ringing: the ideal filter's characteristic defect

The counterintuitive part A hard cutoff in the frequency domain does not produce a hard, clean result in the spatial domain — it produces ringing: faint, concentric echo-like ripples radiating outward from every edge. The Convolution Theorem (Section 1.3) explains why: multiplying by H(u,v) in frequency space is the same as convolving the image with H's spatial-domain counterpart. The ideal filter's spatial-domain version is a sinc-like function with a big central lobe (which causes the blurring) surrounded by smaller side-lobes that alternate sign (which cause the ringing) — a mathematical consequence of forcing an abrupt jump from 1 to 0 in the frequency domain. Every pixel effectively gets a small ripple-copy of that sinc function stamped down on it, and neighboring ripples interfere to produce the visible rings around edges.

3.4 · The Gaussian Low-Pass Filter

H(u,v) = e −D²(u,v) / 2D₀²

Instead of a hard wall, the Gaussian filter uses a smooth bell curve as a function of distance from the center — H(u,v) starts at 1 for D = 0 and decays smoothly and continuously toward 0 as D grows, never making a sudden jump anywhere. D₀ plays the role of the Gaussian's standard deviation (σ = D₀), setting how quickly the bell curve falls off.

The counterintuitive part The Gaussian is one of the very few functions whose Fourier transform is also a Gaussian — a smooth bell curve in the frequency domain corresponds to a smooth bell curve in the spatial domain, with no side-lobes at all. That is precisely why Gaussian filtering produces zero ringing: there is no oscillating sinc-like tail to convolve with. The price is a less sharply-defined cutoff — "low-pass" here means "mostly passes low frequencies and gradually attenuates higher ones," not "passes everything below D₀ and nothing above it."

3.5 · The Butterworth Low-Pass Filter

Deck 3, slide 12 — Butterworth LPF (order 2) results at cutoff radii 5, 15, 30, 80, 230, mirroring the ideal/Gaussian result grids above.
H(u,v) = 1 / [ 1 + (D(u,v)/D₀) 2n ]

The Butterworth filter is a tunable middle ground between the previous two extremes, controlled by its order n:

  • As n grows large, the transition from 1 to 0 gets steeper and steeper, and the filter's behavior converges toward the ideal filter's hard cutoff.
  • As n shrinks toward 1, the transition softens and the filter's behavior approaches the Gaussian's smooth roll-off.

The spatial-domain kernels make the trade-off visible directly: order-1 has no ringing and no negative values at all; order-2 shows only mild, usually imperceptible ringing; by order-20 the kernel looks essentially identical to the ideal filter's ringing sinc pattern. This gives a practical rule of thumb the slides state directly: orders 2–3 are the sweet spot — steep enough to filter effectively, gentle enough that ringing stays negligible.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit All three filters are ultimately trading off the same three properties against each other, and no filter shape escapes the trade-off — it only chooses where to sit on it:

This is why Butterworth filters are generally preferred in real systems: Ideal and Gaussian are the two fixed endpoints of a spectrum that Butterworth lets you dial to anywhere in between.
