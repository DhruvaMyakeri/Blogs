Matrix-Based Transforms & Basis Images

Everything so far — the Fourier transform, the DFT, every low/high-pass filter — has been one specific transform. This section pulls the camera back and asks: what do all of these techniques actually have in common, mathematically? The answer turns every transform in this unit (Fourier, and soon Haar, DCT, DST, wavelets) into a single, reusable template: pick a matrix, multiply.

6.1 · The linear-algebra vocabulary underneath every transform

A handful of definitions get reused constantly from here to the end of the unit, so it's worth being precise about each one before the machinery starts moving:

  • Vector space — any collection of objects that can be added together and scaled, and still land back inside the same collection. An image row, an entire image, and a plain arrow in 2D space are all "vectors" in this sense.
  • Inner product — a rule ⟨u,v⟩ for combining two vectors into a single number, obeying a short list of sane axioms (symmetry under conjugation, linearity, positivity). The ordinary dot product is the most familiar example.
‖z‖ = √⟨z,z⟩   (length / norm)      θ = cos−1( ⟨z,w⟩ / ‖z‖‖w‖ )   (angle between two vectors)

Two vectors are orthogonal when ⟨z,w⟩ = 0, i.e. θ = 90°. This single fact — "orthogonal means zero inner product" — is the fact every transform in this unit is built to exploit.

Basis vectors: describing any vector as a recipe of "how much of each ingredient"

A basis {w₀, w₁, w₂, …} for a vector space is a set of vectors from which every vector z in the space can be built as a weighted sum z = α₀w₀ + α₁w₁ + α₂w₂ + …. If the basis vectors are pairwise orthogonal and each has length 1 (⟨wk,wl⟩ = δkl, the Kronecker delta — 1 when k = l, 0 otherwise), the basis is called orthonormal.

The counterintuitive part Section 1.4 defined the DFT coefficient F(u) via an explicit correlation sum, and it looked like a special trick specific to sinusoids. It wasn't. Watch what happens when you take the inner product of z with one particular basis vector wi:
⟨wi, z⟩ = ⟨wi, α₀w₀ + α₁w₁ + …⟩ = α₀⟨wi,w₀⟩ + α₁⟨wi,w₁⟩ + …

Because the basis is orthonormal, every single term on the right is zero except the one where the index matches i (since ⟨wi,wi⟩ = 1) — every other term vanishes by definition of orthogonality. The whole sum collapses to just αi:

αi = ⟨wi, z⟩

This is exactly Section 1.4's "how much of frequency u is present" question, generalized completely: for any orthonormal basis, the coefficient of the i-th basis vector is found the same way — take the inner product of the signal with that one basis vector. The DFT's "multiply by a reference wave and sum" was never special to sinusoids; it is what you always do once you've chosen an orthonormal basis. Haar, DCT, and DST (Section 7) are just different choices of basis plugged into this identical formula.

6.2 · Every transform is one matrix multiplication

T(u) = Σx=0N−1 f(x)·r(x,u)   (forward)      f(x) = Σu=0N−1 T(u)·s(x,u)   (inverse)

This is the general template every 1D transform in this course fits into. r(x,u) and s(x,u) — the forward and inverse transformation kernels — depend only on the indices x and u, never on the actual pixel values. Swap out r and s for a different formula and you get a completely different transform (Fourier, Haar, DCT, …) using the exact same two-line template.

The inverse-transform sum is literally Section 6.1's "z = α₀w₀ + α₁w₁ + …" recipe again: T(i) plays the role of the coefficient αi, and s(x,i) plays the role of the i-th basis vector wi. Whenever s(x,u) happens to be an orthonormal basis, Section 6.1's result applies directly: T(u) = ⟨s(x,u), f(x)⟩.

Packing it all into one matrix multiply

Stack every basis vector su as a row of a matrix A. Then computing all N coefficients T(u) at once — for every u simultaneously — is nothing more than one matrix-vector product:

t = Af
f = ATt   (since AAT = I when A's rows are orthonormal)

t = Af and f = ATt together are the matrix-based transform pair. Because A's rows are real and orthonormal, this is called an orthogonal transform, and it has a genuinely useful physical property: it preserves inner products, lengths, and angles between vectors — ⟨f₁,f₂⟩ = ⟨t₁,t₂⟩ = ⟨Af₁,Af₂⟩. Transforming a signal doesn't stretch, shrink, or distort it in any way that a dot product could detect; it only re-expresses the same vector in a different, rotated coordinate system.

Extending to 2D: images

A forward kernel is separable if it factors into two independent 1D kernels, r(x,y,u,v) = r₁(x,u)·r₂(y,v) — one that acts along rows, one along columns — and symmetric if those two factors are the same function. Every transform used in this unit (Fourier, Haar, DCT, DST) is both. When that holds, the 2D transform pair collapses to:

T = A F AT      F = AT T A
Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit Read T = AFAT operationally, not just algebraically: multiplying F by A on the left transforms every column of the image (a 1D transform run down each column), and multiplying by AT on the right transforms every row of whatever came out of that first step. This is exactly how a 2D FFT is actually implemented in practice — as a batch of 1D FFTs along the rows, followed by a batch of 1D FFTs along the columns — rather than ever building or multiplying by an enormous single matrix for the whole image at once. Section 6's abstract "T = AFAT" is the mathematical justification for that row-then-column engineering trick.
Deck 6, slide 13 — the same formula generalized to non-square M×N images: T = A_M F A_N^T, F = A_M^T T A_N.

6.3 · Complex and biorthogonal bases

The Fourier transform's basis vectors are complex-valued (recall e−j2πux/M from Section 1.4), so the real-valued machinery above needs one small patch: replace the transpose with a conjugate transpose everywhere. A matrix built from complex orthonormal basis vectors is called unitary, and its defining property A−1 = A*T is the direct complex-number analogue of A−1 = AT for real orthogonal matrices.

The counterintuitive part A basis doesn't strictly need to be orthonormal to be invertible and useful. A biorthogonal basis {s₀,s₁,…} works with a separate, paired-up "dual" basis {s̃₀,s̃₁,…} — the two sets aren't orthogonal to themselves, only to each other (⟨s̃k, sl⟩ = δkl). This relaxation matters later: several practical wavelet families used in image compression are biorthogonal rather than strictly orthonormal, because it turns out to be easier to design a good compression wavelet if you're not forced to make the analysis and synthesis basis identical.

Deck 6 poses (without fully solving) a small worked exercise here: given the 2-element basis s₀ = (1/√2)[1,1]T and s₁ = (1/√2)[1,−1]T, find the 2×2 transform matrix A and its inverse.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit Worth finishing by hand, because it turns out to be one of the most important small matrices in the whole unit. Stacking s₀ and s₁ as rows gives A = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [1, −1]]. Check orthonormality directly: ⟨s₀,s₀⟩ = ½(1+1) = 1, ⟨s₁,s₁⟩ = ½(1+1) = 1, and ⟨s₀,s₁⟩ = ½(1−1) = 0 — so A's rows are indeed orthonormal, and since A is also symmetric here, A−1 = AT = A. For any input f = [f₀, f₁]T:
t = Af = (1/√2) [ f₀ + f₁ ,   f₀ − f₁ ]T
In words: the first output coefficient is (a scaled) sum of the two inputs, and the second is (a scaled) difference. Sum-and-difference on pairs of samples is precisely the operation Section 7.1 introduces as the Haar transform — so this "toy" 2×2 example isn't a random linear-algebra drill, it's the smallest possible Haar transform, derived here from pure orthonormal-basis reasoning before Section 7 ever gives it a name.

6.4 · Basis images

Just as a 1D signal is a weighted sum of 1D basis vectors, a 2D image can be written as a weighted sum of 2D basis images Su,v — one full-size image-shaped pattern per (u,v) coefficient, built from the outer product of two 1D basis vectors: Su,v = susvT. Each T(u,v) coefficient is then simply "how much of that one specific pattern is present" in the image being transformed.

This 8-point Fourier transform matrix (built from powers of ω = e−j2π/8) generates the checkerboard gallery of basis images beside it — the real and imaginary parts of every susvT outer product, arranged in a grid. Notice the pattern: the top-left tile (u=v=0) is flat gray — the DC basis image, pure average brightness with no oscillation at all, exactly as Section 1.4 described F(0). Moving right or down through the grid, the stripes get progressively finer — each tile is a literal picture of "how fast this particular frequency oscillates," laid out as an image instead of described in words. You will see this same gallery-of-basis-images idea again in concrete, hands-on form in Section 7 for the Haar and DCT transforms.

Why go to all this trouble? Three properties worth the abstraction

  • Energy conservation — an orthonormal/unitary transform is just a rotation of coordinates, so it cannot create or destroy total signal energy; ‖f‖² = ‖t‖² always.
  • Energy compaction — a well-chosen basis packs most of that (conserved) energy into a small number of coefficients, leaving the rest near zero. This is exactly the phenomenon Section 1.5 demonstrated concretely (keeping 1% of DFT coefficients still kept 98.8% of sample.png's energy) — Section 6 is naming and generalizing what that experiment already showed.
  • Decorrelation — pixel values in a natural image are highly correlated with their neighbors (a pixel and the one next to it are usually similar). A good transform's coefficients are much less correlated with each other than the original pixels were, even though no information was lost. This matters enormously for compression: Section 9's Huffman coding gets far more benefit out of decorrelated, energy-compacted transform coefficients than it ever could out of raw, highly-correlated pixel values — which is the entire reason a transform step comes before the entropy-coding step in every compression pipeline in Sections 8–11.
