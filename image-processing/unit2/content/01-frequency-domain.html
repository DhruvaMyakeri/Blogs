Frequency Domain & the Fourier Transform

Every image you have ever looked at can be redescribed, with zero loss of information, as a sum of waves. That sentence sounds like a trick — it is not. This section builds the idea up from scratch: what "frequency" even means for something that is not a sound, why Fourier's 200-year-old claim about periodic functions applies to a photograph, and how the continuous idea gets turned into something a computer can actually compute (the DFT).

1.1 · What does "frequency" mean in an image?

In audio, frequency is easy to picture: a signal that oscillates quickly (say, 5000 times a second) is "high frequency" — a whistle. A signal that oscillates slowly is "low frequency" — a bass hum. An image has no time axis, so where would frequency even come from?

The trick is to replace time with space. Instead of asking "how fast does the air pressure oscillate as time passes," we ask "how fast does the pixel intensity oscillate as you move across the image." That is the entire definition. Nothing about color, brightness level, or content — purely: how quickly does gray-level change as you slide along a row or column of pixels.

The classic demonstration: split a photograph into a low-frequency version (the smooth blur on the left) and a high-frequency version (the edge-only sketch on the right). Neither half alone is the photo — but between the two of them, every bit of the original is accounted for. This is the same low/high split previewed with sample.png at the end of this section, just with a face instead of a gradient/checkerboard.

Deck 1, slide 3 — a smooth left-to-right gradient

Low spatial frequency = slow change

Look at the gradient square. Walk one scan line straight across it (the red line) and plot the intensity you see, and you get slide 4's curve: a smooth, gently rising ramp from black to white. Nothing jumps. Neighboring pixels are always close in value. That gradual rate of change is what "low spatial frequency" means — not "dim" or "gray," but slowly-varying.

Deck 1, slide 5 — stripes, checkerboards, zig-zags

High spatial frequency = fast change

Now scan across the checkerboard. The intensity signal you get (slide 6) is a square wave slamming between 0 and 255 every few pixels. That rapid back-and-forth is "high spatial frequency." Sharp edges, fine texture, noise, and thin repeated patterns all live here, because all of them force intensity to change over a very short pixel distance.

The counterintuitive part "Frequency" in an image is not a property of one pixel — it only exists as a description of a neighborhood. A single mid-gray pixel carries no frequency information at all; frequency is about how that pixel's value relates to the pixels around it. This is why frequency-domain reasoning always talks about the whole image (or at least a whole patch) at once, never a single sample in isolation.

Real photographs mix both, everywhere, at once

A real image is not purely smooth or purely stripy — every photograph is a blend of both extremes at every location. Skin and sky are low-frequency (values barely change pixel to pixel); hair strands, fabric weave, and eyelashes are high-frequency (values swing wildly pixel to pixel), and they sit right next to each other in the same photo.

Slide 8 makes this concrete with an intensity trace along one scan line of a real portrait: the flat stretches over the forehead and background are the low-frequency parts of the signal, and every sharp notch (hairline, eyebrow, iris, lips, jacket edge) is a burst of high frequency. A Fourier transform is simply a tool that takes a signal like this and reports how much of each rate-of-change is present, without caring where in the image it occurred.

1.2 · Fourier's claim: any periodic signal is a sum of sine waves

Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier's claim, stripped of notation, is startling: take any periodic function — no matter how jagged, asymmetric, or strange — and it can be built exactly by adding up plain sine and cosine waves of different frequencies, each scaled by its own coefficient.

Slide 9's stack makes the claim visually undeniable: the top few rows are pure, boring sine waves at increasing frequency. Add enough of them together with the right weights, and the bottom row — a genuinely irregular, complicated-looking signal — falls out. That bottom signal is not "kind of like" a sum of sines; it is exactly the sum, no approximation needed (for a periodic signal, given infinitely many terms).

Fourier series: the formula behind the claim

f(t) = Σn=−∞∞ cn e j·2πnt/T     where  cn = (1/T) ∫−T/2T/2 f(t) e −j·2πnt/T dt

Read the two pieces separately, because each answers a different question:

  • The sum says: f(t) is built from an infinite ladder of sinusoids at frequencies that are whole-number multiples (harmonics) of the fundamental 1/T.
  • The integral for cn says: to find out how much of harmonic n is present in f(t), multiply f(t) by that harmonic's own wave and integrate (sum up) the result over one period. This "multiply-and-integrate" trick is called correlation, and it is the single most important computational idea in this entire unit — every transform in this course (DFT, DCT, Haar, wavelets) is a different family of "reference waves" that you correlate the signal against.

The ejθ notation is Euler's shorthand for cos θ + j·sin θ — so A·sin(ωx + φ) with amplitude A, angular frequency ω, and phase angle φ is exactly the same physical wave, just written differently. Both forms appear interchangeably on the slides.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit Notice cn is generally a complex number, not just an amplitude. That is not a mathematical inconvenience — the complex value simultaneously encodes two real numbers: how much of that frequency is present (its magnitude, |cn|) and where the wave is shifted to (its phase, ∠cn). When you later see a "Fourier spectrum" image, you are looking at only the magnitude half of this information — the phase is thrown away for visualization, even though the phase is at least as important for reconstructing the image (a classic demonstration: swap the phase of two unrelated images while keeping their magnitudes, and the result looks like the phase-donor image, not the magnitude-donor).

From a periodic sum to a continuous transform

The Fourier series only works if f(t) is periodic. Most real signals — including a single photograph — are not periodic at all. Fourier's transform is the generalization that removes this restriction, by letting the period T grow to infinity, which turns the discrete sum over harmonics into a continuous integral over every possible frequency μ:

Forward:   F(μ) = ∫−∞∞ f(t) e −j·2πμt dt
Inverse:   f(t) = ∫−∞∞ F(μ) e j·2πμt dμ

F(μ) and f(t) are called a Fourier transform pair — each is a complete, lossless redescription of the other. Nothing is thrown away going from f(t) to F(μ); you could run the inverse transform and get f(t) back exactly. This reversibility is what makes "go to the frequency domain, do something useful, come back" a safe and popular strategy throughout the rest of this unit.

1.3 · Why bother? The computational payoff

Before wading further into the mathematics, it is worth answering the practical question: why would anyone convert an image into the frequency domain just to filter it, instead of filtering it directly with a spatial kernel (as you would have done in Unit 1)?

For an M×N image filtered with an m×n kernel:

  • Spatial-domain convolution costs on the order of M·N·m·n operations for a general (non-separable) kernel — every output pixel needs its own little weighted sum over the whole kernel footprint.
  • Frequency-domain filtering costs on the order of 2·M·N·log₂(M·N) operations — the 2 is because you need one forward FFT and one inverse FFT.

The crossover shows up exactly where you would expect: for small kernels, plain spatial convolution is cheaper (less overhead). But m·n grows quadratically with kernel size, while log₂(M·N) barely grows at all — so once a kernel gets large, FFT-based filtering wins by an increasingly wide margin. This is why, for example, large-radius blurs in image-editing software are typically implemented via the FFT rather than direct convolution.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit This entire efficiency argument is built on a fact the deck uses constantly from Section 3 onward but never actually states: the Convolution Theorem. It says convolution in the spatial domain is exactly equivalent to plain multiplication in the frequency domain:
f(x,y) ✳ h(x,y)  ⟺  F(u,v) · H(u,v)
where ✳ is spatial convolution and ⟺ marks a Fourier transform pair. This single fact is why frequency-domain filtering is possible at all: "filtering" (convolving with a kernel h) in the spatial domain becomes "multiply every frequency component by a number H(u,v)" in the frequency domain — no sliding windows, no per-pixel sums, just one multiplication per frequency. Every low-pass/high-pass filter you will meet in Sections 3–5 is defined as an H(u,v) precisely because of this equivalence.

1.4 · From continuous to discrete: the DFT

Deck 1, slide 14 — section divider marking the shift from the continuous Fourier transform (§1.2–1.3) to its discrete counterpart.

A digital image is not a continuous function — it is a finite grid of samples. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is the version of the Fourier transform built for exactly that setting: a finite number of samples in, a finite number of frequency components out, with no integrals anywhere (just finite sums, which a computer can execute directly).

Forward DFT:   F(u) = Σx=0M−1 f(x) e −j·2π·ux/M    for u = 0,1,…,M−1
Inverse DFT:   f(x) = (1/M) Σu=0M−1 F(u) e j·2π·ux/M    for x = 0,1,…,M−1

Same idea as the continuous transform — correlate the signal against reference waves of every candidate frequency — just restricted to M discrete samples and M discrete frequency slots. The slides use a small notation convention worth pinning down, because it recurs for the rest of the unit:

SymbolUsed for
m, ndiscrete dummy variables inside derivations
x, yspatial (pixel) coordinates
u, vfrequency-domain coordinates

What is the DFT actually asking? ("Varying u")

The formula is compact but opaque on first read. The most useful way to actually understand it is to freeze everything except u and watch what happens as u increases one step at a time — this is the single best intuition-building slide in the whole deck.

  • u = 0: the reference wave e−j2π(0)x/M collapses to exactly 1 for every x (no oscillation at all). So F(0) = Σ f(x) — literally just the sum of every pixel value. Divide by M and you get the plain average brightness of the whole image. This is called the DC component (a term borrowed from electronics: "direct current," i.e. the zero-frequency, non-oscillating part of a signal), and it encodes overall illumination — nothing about shape or detail.
  • u = 1: the reference wave now completes exactly one full oscillation across the whole signal. F(1) answers "how much does the image resemble one slow wave stretched across its full width?" — this is the lowest non-trivial frequency.
  • u = 2, 3, …: the reference wave packs in more and more oscillations. Larger u values are correlated against faster wiggling references, so they pick out finer and finer spatial detail — by u = 7 (for an 8-sample signal) you are testing for the fastest possible alternation the sampling grid can represent.
The counterintuitive part Every single F(u) is computed from every single pixel of the input — the sum runs over all of x each time. There is no sense in which "F(u) lives at some location" the way a pixel lives at some location; each frequency coefficient is a global summary statistic of the entire signal. This is exactly why frequency-domain filtering is a whole-image operation and cannot, by itself, target one region of an image (that limitation is part of the motivation for the wavelet and block-transform methods you will meet in Sections 7 and 11, which trade some frequency resolution for spatial locality).

Worked example: computing a 4-point DFT by hand

The abstraction is easier to trust after running it once with real numbers. Take four samples f = [1, 2, 4, 4] (M = 4):

Walking through F(0) and F(1) explicitly:

F(0) = f(0)+f(1)+f(2)+f(3) = 1+2+4+4 = 11  (the DC term — matches "just add everything up")

F(1) = Σ f(x)·e−j2π(1)x/4 = 1·e0 + 2·e−jπ/2 + 4·e−jπ + 4·e−j3π/2 = −3 + 2j

(F(2) and F(3) follow the identical mechanical process with u = 2 and u = 3, giving −(1+0j) and −(3+2j) respectively — the slide states these without re-deriving them since the pattern is now established.)

Running the inverse DFT on the resulting {F(u)} set reconstructs f(0) = 1 exactly:

f(0) = (1/4)·[F(0)+F(1)+F(2)+F(3)] = (1/4)[11 −3+2j −1 −3−2j] = (1/4)[4] = 1 ✓

The imaginary parts cancel out in the sum precisely because f(x) was a real-valued signal to begin with — a useful sanity check to keep in mind when you compute these by hand: if your final f(x) comes out with a leftover imaginary part, a sign or index has gone wrong somewhere in the forward transform.

The DFT is just a matrix multiplication

Every one of those per-u summations can be stacked into a single matrix equation F = W·f, where W is an M×M matrix built entirely from powers of a single complex number W = e−j2π/N (this specific W is sometimes called a twiddle factor). This reframing is what makes the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) possible: the FFT is not a different transform from the DFT — it is the exact same F = W·f matrix multiplication, computed by exploiting repeated structure inside W so the cost drops from the naive O(M²) (a full matrix-vector multiply) down to the O(M log M) figure used in the efficiency argument back in Section 1.3.

1.5 · From 1D to 2D: transforming an actual image

An image is a 2D grid, so it needs a frequency coordinate in each direction: u for the horizontal direction, v for the vertical. The 2D DFT is simply the 1D idea applied along both axes at once:

F(u,v) = Σx=0M−1 Σy=0N−1 f(x,y) e −j2π(ux/M + vy/N)
f(x,y) = (1/MN) Σu=0M−1 Σv=0N−1 F(u,v) e j2π(ux/M + vy/N)

f(x,y) is the M×N image; F(u,v) is its 2D spectrum — a grid of the same size, where each entry says how strongly the image resembles a 2D sinusoidal grating oscillating at rate u along x and rate v along y.

Worked example: why a set of stripes becomes exactly three dots

Deck 2 revisits the 2D DFT with a worked example that is worth studying closely, because it is the cleanest possible bridge between the formula and an actual spectrum image.

A single 1D cosine wave has a 1D spectrum |F(k)| made of three spikes: one at the DC position (k=0) and two symmetric spikes at ±k₀, where k₀ is the wave's own frequency. Stretch that cosine into vertical stripes (a 2D pattern that only varies along x, not along y) and the 2D spectrum is the same three-spike story, just laid out along the kx axis: one dot at the origin, and two dots straddling it symmetrically.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit The slide poses "what do the three dots correspond to?" as an open question, so it's worth answering directly. A single cosine cos(2πk₀x) is not a single-frequency signal in the complex-exponential sense the DFT uses — by Euler's formula, cos(2πk₀x) = ½ej2πk₀x + ½e−j2πk₀x, i.e. it is the sum of a positive-frequency and a negative-frequency component in equal measure. That gives two of the three dots. The third, at the very center, is the DC term: the stripe pattern's average brightness, exactly as in the 1D case. This ±k₀ mirroring is not specific to this one example — any real-valued image has a spectrum that is conjugate-symmetric about the origin (F(−u,−v) = F*(u,v)), so every spectrum you ever look at in this unit will show this same mirrored structure.

Two things to notice, both direct consequences of what you just derived above. First, the spectrum is symmetric about the exact center — flip it 180° and it looks the same — which is the F(−u,−v) = F*(u,v) conjugate-symmetry property showing up in a real photograph instead of a toy cosine. Second, almost all of the energy is crammed into the bright blob and cross right at the center: real photographs are overwhelmingly low-frequency, because most of any photo is smoothly-shaded regions rather than stripes. The faint bright cross running exactly horizontal and vertical through the center is a give-away artifact of the image's own rectangular border and any strong horizontal/vertical edges in the scene (here, the frame edges and the horizon-like tonal boundary) — the DFT implicitly assumes the image tiles infinitely, so a sharp left/right or top/bottom edge mismatch behaves like an added high-frequency step function along that one axis.

New here: the sampling ↔ frequency-resolution trade-off

Slide 27 introduces one genuinely new relationship worth pulling out on its own, because Section 2 leans on it heavily: if f(x) consists of M samples spaced ΔT apart, the record length is T = M·ΔT, and the resulting spacing between frequency bins is Δu = 1/(M·ΔT) = 1/T.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit Read this as a trade-off, not just a formula: to get finer frequency resolution (smaller Δu, i.e. the ability to tell two nearby frequencies apart), you must either sample for a longer record length T, or take more samples M at the same spacing. You cannot get high resolution in both space and frequency for free — this is a discrete-signal cousin of the uncertainty principle, and it is part of the motivation for the wavelet transform in Section 7, which is specifically designed to trade some frequency resolution back for spatial locality.
