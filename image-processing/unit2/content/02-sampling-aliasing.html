Sampling, Aliasing & Interpolation

Section 1 established that frequency is a real, measurable property of an image. This section asks a sharper question: when a camera (or a scanner, or a resize operation) turns a continuous scene into a finite grid of pixels, what can go wrong — and why is the answer sometimes "the resulting image looks completely normal, and is completely wrong"?

2.1 · What is aliasing?

Deck 2, slide 2 — section divider.

Every digital image starts life as a continuous signal — light hitting a sensor — that gets sampled at discrete points. Sampling is inherently a lossy step: you are throwing away everything that happened between the sample points and hoping it didn't matter. Aliasing is the name for what happens when it did matter: fine, repetitive detail that changes faster than the sampling grid can track gets misread as a completely different, usually coarser, pattern.

The tell-tale wavy interference pattern on the round tower above is not something that existed on the real building — the bricks are laid in a perfectly regular grid. It is an artifact created purely by the act of photographing that regular brick pattern at a sampling rate that could not keep up with it.

Aliasing: two or more different continuous signals become identical once sampled.

Slide 6 makes the definition precise with the cleanest possible example: two genuinely different sine waves, sampled at the same (too-slow) rate, produce the exact same sequence of sample points. Once you only have the samples, there is no way to tell which of the two original waves you started with — the information distinguishing them was destroyed at the moment of sampling, not lost afterward. This pair of indistinguishable signals is called an aliased pair.

2.2 · The Sampling Theorem and the Nyquist rate

1/ΔT > 2·μmax

In plain language: if a signal's fastest-oscillating component has frequency μmax, you must take samples at a rate exceeding twice that frequency to have any hope of perfectly reconstructing the original signal from the samples. Exactly twice μmax is called the Nyquist rate — a name worth knowing, because it is the single most load-bearing term in this whole section.

The counterintuitive part You might expect that sampling exactly at the Nyquist rate is always enough. The slide is careful to say it is only "sometimes sufficient" — there are edge cases (for instance, sampling a sine wave exactly at its own peaks and troughs happens to work, but sampling it exactly at its zero-crossings would recover a flat line). This is precisely why the theorem is stated with a strict inequality (exceeding twice the frequency), not "greater than or equal to" — real systems build in a safety margin above the Nyquist rate rather than sampling exactly at it.

The bottom three panels of slide 4 show the frequency-domain mechanics behind recovery: sampling a continuous band-limited signal makes its spectrum repeat infinitely (top panel — this periodic repetition is a direct mathematical consequence of sampling, not something specific to images). An ideal low-pass filter (middle panel) can cut out exactly one of those repeated copies — but only if the copies are spaced far enough apart not to overlap. Sample too slowly and the repeated copies overlap each other, and no filter, however good, can separate them back out again. That overlap is aliasing, seen from the frequency domain rather than the "two sine waves look the same" spatial-domain view above.

What this means for images specifically

Images bring two flavors of the same underlying problem:

  • Spatial aliasing — caused by under-sampling a single frame; shows up most in images with fine repeating patterns (brick walls, fabric weaves, window screens).
  • Temporal aliasing — the same idea along the time axis of a video, when the frame rate is too slow relative to how fast something moves. The classic example is the "wagon-wheel effect": a stagecoach wheel filmed at 24 frames per second can appear to rotate slowly backward, because each frame happens to catch a spoke very close to (but not exactly at) the position of the previous spoke — the camera's frame rate is aliasing the wheel's true rotation rate down to a much slower, even reversed, apparent rate.

2.3 · Seeing spatial aliasing happen

Overlay a fixed sampling grid on regions of increasing stripe frequency, and the behavior is not uniform:

  • Low-frequency region — the grid samples the stripes plenty of times per cycle; the reconstructed edges are only mildly jagged.
  • Mid-frequency region — fewer samples per cycle, so jaggedness increases even though the sampling rate hasn't changed — only the signal's own frequency has.
  • High-frequency region — the stripes now oscillate faster than the sampling grid can track. The reconstruction doesn't just get "worse" in the way blur gets worse — it produces entirely new, lower-frequency stripes, rotated relative to the originals, that were never in the source at all.
The counterintuitive part Aliasing does not degrade gracefully the way noise or blur does. A blurred image still obviously resembles the original, just softer. An aliased image can look perfectly sharp, perfectly clean, and perfectly plausible — while showing you a pattern that has no relationship whatsoever to the real scene. "It looks fine" is not evidence that an image was sampled correctly.

2.4 · Why you can't fix aliasing after the fact

Deck 2, slide 9 — recap: aliased functions can bear no resemblance to the original; fix by smoothing before sampling (anti-aliasing).

Once the overlapping copies in the frequency domain have merged (Section 2.2's overlap), the information needed to tell them apart is gone from the samples — no amount of post-processing can un-merge them, because post-processing can only operate on the samples you actually have. This is why the fix has to happen before sampling: pre-filter (defocus / low-pass) the continuous signal so that its own μmax already respects the Nyquist limit of the sampling rate you're about to use. This is called anti-aliasing, and the name is precise about timing — it is a preventative step, not a cure.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit This resolves a naming confusion the slide flags but doesn't fully untangle: image-editing software often has a button literally labeled "anti-aliasing," and it does help — but it is solving a different, second sampling problem (the one introduced by resampling the software's own internal representation while resizing/rotating), not the sampling that already happened when the original photograph's continuous light hit the discrete sensor grid. The first aliasing event is permanent and unrecoverable; the software-level control only prevents new aliasing being added on top of it.

This is also exactly why real cameras and even biological eyes build in physical anti-aliasing: an optical low-pass filter over the sensor, or (in the eye) the finite blur introduced by the lens and by the spacing of photoreceptors, both quietly discard frequencies that the downstream sampling grid could not represent safely anyway.

Worked example: the checkerboard that lies to you

With one sample per pixel, the Nyquist limit is exactly ½ cycle/pixel. Walking the square size down:

  • 16×16 and 6×6 pixel squares — comfortably below ½ cycle/pixel → rendered correctly.
  • Squares slightly under 1 pixel — the pattern's true frequency is now far above what one-sample-per-pixel can represent → severe, obviously-wrong aliasing (the grainy noise-like patch).
  • Squares slightly under 0.5 pixels — pushed even further past the limit, the aliasing "wraps around" again and produces a different, perfectly regular-looking checkerboard — one that is entirely fabricated by the sampling process and bears no relationship to the true, much finer pattern.

That last case is the sharpest possible illustration of Section 2.3's counterintuitive point: it is indistinguishable, just by looking at it, from a correctly-sampled image.

2.5 · Image resampling and interpolation

Resizing an image is itself a resampling operation, so everything above applies directly:

  • Zooming (enlarging) is oversampling — you need to invent extra sample points that were never actually captured, via interpolation.
  • Shrinking is undersampling — you are literally reducing the sampling rate below what it was, which is exactly the situation Section 2.2's theorem warns about.

Nearest-neighbor interpolation — the simplest possible zoom, just replicate the nearest known pixel — is cheap but reintroduces aliasing of its own: since it invents no new intermediate values, a diagonal or curved edge in the source becomes a staircase of harshly-bounded blocks ("jaggies") in the enlarged result. Shrinking has the opposite danger: naive shrinking (just deleting pixels, i.e. plain decimation with no pre-filtering) violates the sampling theorem exactly the way the checkerboard example did.

Figure 4.19 nails the fix directly to the theorem: (a) is a normal photo; (b) shrinks it by naive pixel deletion and then restores the original size by pixel replication — the striped scarf now shows obvious, ugly interference; (c) applies an averaging (low-pass) filter before the same shrink-and-restore, and the aliasing disappears, at the cost of a slightly softer image. (c) is anti-aliasing in miniature: exactly the pre-filter-before-you-sample discipline from Section 2.4.

Nearest-neighbor produces exactly the hard, blocky jaggies the slide warns about — every edge in the image is now a staircase. Bilinear (averaging the nearest 2×2 neighborhood) softens those staircases into gradual ramps. Bicubic (a wider, smoother weighted average over a 4×4 neighborhood) goes further still, producing the smoothest edges of the three at the cost of the most computation — the general pattern for every interpolation method in this unit: more neighboring samples consulted, smoother result, higher cost.

2.6 · Moiré patterns

A Moiré pattern is what you get when two regular, fine patterns of nearly the same spacing are superimposed — think two layers of insect window screen, or a striped shirt on a TV camera. The slide's key clarifying point: Moiré is not exclusively a digital sampling artifact. It is a more general phenomenon of any two overlapping periodic gratings, digital or purely optical.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit The caption under the vector-drawing figure says superimposing two patterns "is analogous to multiplying the patterns" — worth making concrete, because it is the same convolution-theorem machinery from Section 1.3. Multiplying two gratings in the spatial domain corresponds (again via the Convolution Theorem, this time in its "multiplication ↔ convolution" direction) to convolving their two spectra in the frequency domain. Convolving two spectra that each have energy concentrated at some frequency f produces new energy at both the sum (2f-ish) and, critically, the difference of the two original frequencies. When the two gratings have nearly equal spacing, that difference frequency is very low — a slow, large-scale pattern is exactly what a "high-frequency minus a very similar high-frequency" produces, which is precisely the coarse wavy pattern you see in a Moiré effect.

Digital sampling then adds its own contribution: sampling a fine, regular print pattern (halftone dots, or any repetitive texture) with a sampling grid of comparable spacing is itself "superimposing two gratings" — the image content and the sampling lattice — so ordinary aliasing and true optical Moiré end up looking and behaving alike, even though the underlying cause is slightly different in each case.

Newspapers print grayscale photos using halftone dots — clusters of pure black dots of varying size and spacing that the eye blends into apparent gray levels. Digitizing (scanning) such a print at a resolution close to the dot spacing beats the two patterns (dot grid × scanner sampling grid) against each other, producing the blotchy interference visible above — a direct, real-world instance of the Section 2.3 "sampling grid vs. pattern frequency" story, and the reason professional scanning of printed halftones uses descreening (a deliberate blur/low-pass filter) before resampling.
