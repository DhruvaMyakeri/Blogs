Lecture 4 · Deck 4

Sharpening Filters in the Frequency Domain

Section 3 built three ways to keep low frequencies and discard high ones. This section simply flips that goal: keep the high frequencies — the edges, the fine texture, the detail — and discard the low ones. Because of that symmetry, most of Section 3's hard-won intuition (ringing, the ideal/Gaussian/Butterworth spectrum) transfers over directly.

4.1 · High-pass = 1 − low-pass

Edges and fine detail correspond to rapid intensity change, which — from Section 1 — is exactly what high spatial frequency means. So sharpening is: build an H(u,v) that suppresses low frequencies and passes high ones. Rather than deriving three new formulas from scratch, notice that "everything except the low frequencies" is just the complement of a low-pass filter:

Hhp(u,v) = 1 − Hlp(u,v)

Every filter from Section 3 therefore has an immediate high-pass counterpart, just by flipping this one sign.

4.2 · Ideal High-Pass Filter

H(u,v) = 0  if D(u,v) ≤ D₀     H(u,v) = 1  if D(u,v) > D₀

A disc of zeros at the center (killing the DC term and everything near it), surrounded by ones everywhere else. Applied to the same multi-element test image used throughout Section 3:

Every flat region collapses to black — with the DC term gone, there is nothing left to represent "average brightness" — while every edge (the boxes, the letter "a", the bar chart, even the noise texture) survives as a bright outline. As D₀ grows, fewer low frequencies are allowed to survive, so the result gets sparser and edge-ier.

The counterintuitive part A high-pass-filtered image is not simply "the original minus the blur" in appearance — it looks like line art. Every region of constant or slowly-varying intensity, no matter how large, contributes essentially nothing, because the DC and near-DC frequencies that encoded it were exactly what got deleted. This is the mirror image of the ideal low-pass result from Section 3.3, which kept the shapes but destroyed the edges.

4.3 · Butterworth and Gaussian High-Pass Filters

H(u,v) = 1 / [ 1 + (D₀ / D(u,v))2n ]
H(u,v) = 1 − e −D²(u,v) / 2D₀²

Same trade-off as Section 3.5, mirrored: the Butterworth transition can be tuned from sharp (large n, ideal-like) to gradual (small n, Gaussian-like) via its order, while the Gaussian version has a fixed, always smooth roll-off.

Comparing all three side by side

At the same D₀ = 15, the ideal filter's result is the crispest but noisiest-looking (ringing again), the Gaussian's is the smoothest but has lost the most fine detail, and Butterworth (order 2) sits in between — exactly the same ranking as the low-pass comparison in Section 3.5.

The 3-D transfer-function plots make the family relationship visually obvious: all three are a bowl-shaped dip to zero at the center, differing only in how abruptly the walls of the bowl rise.

Where the high-pass spatial kernel comes from

hHP(x,y) = 𝔉−1[HHP(u,v)] = 𝔉−1[1 − HLP(u,v)] = δ(x,y) − hLP(x,y)

This uses a small but useful fact: the inverse transform of the constant function "1" is a unit impulse δ(x,y) — a single spike, zero everywhere else. So the high-pass kernel in the spatial domain is literally "a spike, minus the low-pass blur kernel." That is worth sitting with, because it is exactly the unsharp-masking idea from Section 5 stated as a single kernel: subtracting a blurred copy from the original (represented here by the spike, i.e. the unblurred image) is what produces a sharpened result.

The resulting kernels confirm the family relationship one more time: the ideal filter's kernel shows the same ringing side-lobes seen throughout Section 3, while Gaussian and Butterworth taper cleanly to zero — and because a Gaussian's spectrum is broader-skirted than a comparable Butterworth's, its spatial kernel comes out narrower.
