Unsharp Masking, High-Boost & Homomorphic Filtering

Section 4 built pure high-pass filters, which throw away the DC term and every low frequency near it — useful for isolating edges, but the result is dim, featureless line art (Section 4.2's counterintuitive point). This section fixes that by adding high-pass content back onto the original instead of replacing the original with it, then pushes the same idea to its logical extreme with Homomorphic filtering, a technique for fixing bad lighting.

5.1 · Unsharp masking, high-boost, and high-frequency emphasis

Unsharp masking is an old darkroom photography technique, and its frequency-domain version follows the same three steps as the original chemical process: blur a copy, subtract it from the original to isolate what the blur lost, then add that difference back on top.

gmask(x,y) = f(x,y) − fLP(x,y)    where fLP(x,y) = 𝔉−1[HLP(u,v)F(u,v)]

g(x,y) = f(x,y) + k · gmask(x,y)

fLP is just a low-pass-filtered (blurred) copy of the image, built with any of Section 3's filters. gmask — the "mask" — is what that blur removed: exactly the image's own high-frequency content. Adding a scaled copy of that mask back onto the original amplifies the very detail the blur had smoothed away.

  • k = 1 — this is unsharp masking proper.
  • k > 1 — this is high-boost filtering: the same idea, with the high-frequency contribution deliberately over-weighted for a stronger effect.

The mask on its own looks like a faint, contrast-inverted line drawing — almost nothing but edges, at roughly mid-gray everywhere else, which is exactly "the image minus its own smooth part." Adding it back at k = 1 gives a crisper, more defined version of the original with no loss of the original's overall tone. Pushing k up to 4 makes every edge visibly overshoot — a bright halo along the silhouette and fabric folds — which is the well-known visible side-effect of high-boost filtering pushed too far.

Rewriting unsharp masking as a pure frequency-domain filter

Because fLP is itself built from HLP, the whole operation can be collapsed into one filter applied once, with no separate blurring step:

g(x,y) = 𝔉−1{ (1 + k[1 − HLP(u,v)]) F(u,v) }   ≡   g(x,y) = 𝔉−1{ [1 + k·HHP(u,v)] F(u,v) }
Deck 5, slide 3 — the same derivation in the textbook's own equation numbering (4-128 to 4-132).
Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit The rewrite is worth pausing on because it directly answers Section 4's complaint about pure high-pass filtering. HHP alone zeroes the DC term, producing a dim, mean-zero result. Here HHP is multiplied by k and then a flat 1 is added back on top of the whole transfer function. That added 1 passes the original image through completely unchanged at every frequency (since multiplying F(u,v) by 1 changes nothing), while the k·HHP term rides on top of it, boosting high frequencies only. The DC term — and every low frequency — survives because of that added 1; only the high end gets any extra emphasis. This is the entire trick, and it is why the "1 +" is present in every formula on this page.

Generalizing the same idea by decoupling the two constants gives high-frequency-emphasis filtering:

g(x,y) = 𝔉−1{ [k₁ + k₂·HHP(u,v)] F(u,v) }    k₁ ≥ 0, k₂ ≥ 0

k₁ (in place of the fixed "1") controls how much of the original DC/low-frequency content survives, and k₂ independently controls how much high-frequency boost is layered on top. High-boost filtering is the special case k₁ = 1, k₂ = k.

Worked example: a chest X-ray with washed-out contrast

This example (from the textbook) is worth walking through because it shows the technique earning its keep on a real, non-toy image. The X-ray's intensities are crammed into a narrow, dark range — Fig. (a). A Gaussian high-pass filter is used specifically because, per Section 4.3, its spatial kernel has no ringing, and ringing artifacts are unacceptable in a medical image. D₀ = 70 (about 10% of the image's long dimension) was chosen empirically to sharpen boundaries without over-amplifying noise.

  • (b) pure GHPF result — featureless and dark (DC term gone), but the rib boundaries are now crisply delineated — exactly Section 4.2's "line-art" signature.
  • (c) high-frequency-emphasis, k₁ = 0.5, k₂ = 0.75 — tonality is restored (the k₁ term lets the low frequencies back in) with the edge emphasis from (b) layered on top.
  • (d) histogram equalization applied to (c) — a spatial-domain technique (from Unit 1) stacked on top of the frequency-domain result. The slide's own conclusion is a useful methodological takeaway: combining a frequency-domain and a spatial-domain technique gave a better result than either used alone.

5.2 · The Laplacian in the frequency domain

Unit 1's spatial-domain Laplacian sharpening used the familiar 3×3 kernel [0,1,0; 1,−4,1; 0,1,0] — a second-derivative edge detector. That same operator has a clean frequency-domain description: it turns out to be a parabolic bowl, H(u,v) = −4π²(u² + v²) (a pure function of distance from the center, just like every other filter in this unit, just shaped as a downward-curving bowl instead of a cutoff or a bell).

Its inverse DFT reproduces the familiar spatial kernel's structure directly — a bright plus-shaped cross of four positive taps surrounding a strongly negative center, matching the classic [0,1,0;1,−4,1;0,1,0] pattern pixel for pixel once you account for scaling. This is a nice closing of the loop back to Unit 1: the same sharpening operator, derived completely independently from a purely frequency-domain argument, reproduces the exact spatial-domain kernel you'd have written down by hand.

5.3 · Homomorphic filtering

A different, harder problem: correcting for uneven lighting — a photo where one corner is brightly lit and another is in shadow, so the same physical surface reads as very different gray levels depending on where it sits in the frame. Naively brightening the dark areas would also blow out the bright ones; a single global operation cannot fix illumination that varies across the image.

The image formation model that makes this solvable

f(x,y) = i(x,y) · r(x,y)

Any image is modeled as the product of two physically distinct components: illumination i(x,y) — how much light is falling on a point, which typically varies slowly and smoothly across a scene — and reflectance r(x,y) — how much of that light the surface at that point reflects, which changes rapidly wherever the material or texture changes (an edge, a texture boundary). The idea driving everything that follows: illumination lives mostly at low spatial frequency, reflectance lives mostly at high spatial frequency, so if they can be separated, illumination can be suppressed while reflectance is preserved (or even boosted) — correcting the lighting without touching the actual detail.

Why you can't just low/high-pass filter it directly

f(x,y) = i(x,y)·r(x,y)  ⟺  F(u,v) = I(u,v) * R(u,v)
The counterintuitive part Because i and r are multiplied in the spatial domain, the Convolution Theorem (Section 1.3) says their spectra are convolved together in the frequency domain — not simply added or stacked side-by-side. Convolution smears the two spectra into each other, so I(u,v) and R(u,v) cannot be pulled back apart just by drawing a circle in frequency space and filtering: F(u,v)·H(u,v) filters the tangled-up mixture as a whole, not each component independently. Separating a product needs a fundamentally different trick, not just a better-chosen H(u,v).

The trick: logarithms turn multiplication into addition

ln(f(x,y)) = ln(i(x,y)) + ln(r(x,y))

Taking the natural log of both sides converts the product into a sum. Sums of spectra do stay cleanly separated under the Fourier transform (Fourier transforms are linear), so now ordinary frequency filtering becomes valid again — filtering can act differently on the "illumination part" versus the "reflectance part" of the log-image, because they are no longer tangled by a convolution.

The five-step pipeline

  1. ln — take the logarithm of f(x,y), turning the illumination×reflectance product into a sum.
  2. DFT — transform the log-image into the frequency domain: Z(u,v) = Illum(u,v) + Refl(u,v).
  3. Apply H(u,v) — a filter shaped to suppress the low-frequency Illum(u,v) term while preserving or amplifying the high-frequency Refl(u,v) term.
  4. Inverse DFT — back to the spatial domain, giving a sum of a corrected illumination part and a corrected reflectance part.
  5. exp — undo the very first step's logarithm, turning the sum back into a product: g(x,y) = i₀(x,y)·r₀(x,y), the corrected image.

The filter shape H(u,v) needs

γL < 1,   γH > 1

H(u,v) is shaped like a high-pass filter but never actually reaches zero: it sits at a low but nonzero gain γL < 1 near the center (attenuating, not deleting, the illumination-dominated low frequencies) and rises to a gain γH > 1 at high frequencies (amplifying, not just preserving, the reflectance-dominated detail). Both requirements matter: γL < 1 rather than 0 is what distinguishes this from a plain high-pass filter and avoids the "featureless, DC-erased" problem from Section 4.2, while γH > 1 gives a genuine contrast boost to fine detail on top of the illumination fix.
