Haar, DCT, DST and the Wavelet Transform

Section 6 promised that every transform is the same template with a different choice of basis vectors. This section makes good on that promise with three concrete, very different choices — a staircase-shaped basis (Haar), a cosine basis (DCT), and a basis that changes shape as you zoom in (the wavelet transform) — and shows why each one exists.

Deck 7, slide 2 — section divider: "Image Transform: HAAR, DCT, DST, DWT."

7.1 · The Haar transform

Deck 7, slide 4 — formal piecewise definition: h₀(x)=1 everywhere; for u>0, h_u(x) is ±2^(p/2) on two half-intervals and 0 elsewhere.

The Haar functions hu(x) are the basis vectors here, and the formula looks intimidating mainly because of the u = 2p + q indexing — but the shape it produces is simple: h₀(x) = 1 everywhere (the DC/average term, exactly as in every transform so far), and every hu(x) for u > 0 is a single rectangular pulse — +2p/2 over one half of a sub-interval, −2p/2 over the other half, and zero everywhere else. p controls how wide that pulse is (bigger p, narrower pulse — more detail), and q controls where along x it sits.

The N = 8 basis functions above make the pattern vivid: the top row is the flat DC term; the next row is one wide plus/minus pulse spanning half the signal; below that, two narrower pulses side by side; below that, four pulses narrower still. Each level of narrowing doubles how many pulses fit, and each pulse is a local plus/minus comparison — literally "is this half of this small region brighter or darker than the other half?" This is the single biggest conceptual difference from every Fourier-family transform in Sections 1–5: a Fourier basis vector oscillates across the entire signal, while a Haar basis vector (other than h₀) is zero almost everywhere and only asks its question over one small, specific patch.

The matrix, and the connection back to Section 6

Deck 7, slide 7 — the full N=8 Haar matrix A_H and its basis images (Fig. 7.18), the direct 1-level-deeper extension of the N=4 matrix above.
Closing the loop from Section 6.3 The N = 2 Haar matrix is AH = (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]] — exactly the matrix worked out by hand in Section 6.3 from pure orthonormal-basis reasoning, before it had a name. It really is the smallest possible Haar transform. The N = 4 matrix above extends the same idea recursively: row 1 is the global average (h₀), row 2 is one coarse plus/minus split across the whole signal, and rows 3–4 are two finer, non-overlapping plus/minus splits, each covering only half of the signal. Every larger Haar matrix is built the same recursive way.

Worked 1D example: the averaging-and-differencing cascade

This is the most concrete way to actually compute a Haar transform, and it matches the recursive matrix structure exactly. Starting from the 8-sample signal [9, 7, 3, 5, 6, 10, 2, 6]:

  1. Level 1 — pair up neighbors and take their average and (half-)difference: (9,7) → average 8, diff 1; (3,5) → average 4, diff −1; (6,10) → average 8, diff 2; (2,6) → average 4, diff −2.
  2. Level 2 — repeat on the 4 averages [8,4,8,4]: (8,4) → average 6, diff 2; (8,4) → average 6, diff 2.
  3. Level 3 — repeat on the 2 remaining averages [6,6]: average 6, diff 0.
Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit The slide asks "what is necessary to complete the representation?" without answering it. The answer: you need every difference produced along the way, plus the very last, fully-collapsed average — one number that never got split any further. Collect them in coarsest-to-finest order and you get the slide's own stated final output: [6, 0, 0, 2, 2, 1, −1, −2, −2] — wait, that's 9 numbers for 8 original samples, so look again at the ordering actually used: the single top-level average (6), the single top-level diff (0), the two level-2 diffs (2, 2), then the four level-1 diffs (1, −1, −2, −2) — 1 + 1 + 2 + 4 = 8 numbers total, matching the 8 inputs exactly, with zero redundancy. This is the defining shape of a Haar/wavelet transform's output: one slowly-updated "current best average" plus a cascade of ever-finer, ever more numerous correction terms — the same multi-resolution idea that reappears in Section 7.4's wavelet pyramid.

Worked 2D example: what each quadrant of a Haar transform actually shows

Apply the same row-then-column averaging/differencing to a small 8×8 image containing a soft white blob on a black background. The output splits cleanly into four quadrants, and each one has a distinct, readable meaning: the top-left quadrant (143s) is a smaller, blurrier copy of the original blob — the "keep averaging" branch. The top-right and bottom-left quadrants (±48, ±16) light up exactly where the blob has a vertical or horizontal edge — the "differencing" branches responding to a boundary. The bottom-right quadrant is silent here because this particular blob has no strong diagonal structure.

This artist's-impression figure generalizes that same reading, quadrant by quadrant: a plain blob becomes a smaller blob (top-left); a pair of vertical bars becomes a pair of vertical bars (top-right — vertical detail survives in the vertical-detail quadrant); horizontal bars become horizontal bars (bottom-left); and the four corners of a shape — pure diagonal information — become four faint dots in the bottom-right quadrant. This four-way split (smooth / vertical-edge / horizontal-edge / diagonal-edge) is exactly the "2D channel bank" idea, and it is precisely what Section 7.4's wavelet transform formalizes and generalizes.

Properties, honestly stated

The Haar transform is real, orthogonal, and very fast to compute (its recursive averaging/differencing structure runs in O(N) time, faster even than the FFT's O(N log N)). But the slide is direct about its weakness: it has poor energy compaction for natural images compared to the DCT covered next — its blocky, sharp-edged basis functions are a poor match for images that are mostly smooth with occasional edges, so more Haar coefficients are usually needed to represent the same image well.

7.2 · The Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)

s(x,u) = α(u)·cos[ (2x+1)uπ / 2N ]    where α(0)=√(1/N), α(u)=√(2/N) for u>0

Where Haar's basis vectors are local rectangular pulses, the DCT's basis vectors are global cosine waves — much closer in spirit to the Fourier basis than to Haar. The listed properties are worth reading against Section 7.1's Haar properties directly: real, orthogonal, but not symmetric (r₁ ≠ r₂ in Section 6.2's sense along the two axes for the general kernel, though the transform matrix itself is used symmetrically); the same frequency range as the DFT but twice the frequency resolution; excellent energy compaction; and no DC bias issue the way a naive cosine-only expansion might suggest.

The basis-image gallery shows the family resemblance to the DFT's own basis-image gallery from Section 6.4 — smooth in the top-left corner, progressively finer checkerboard patterns moving away from it — with the first row reading as pure vertical lines and the first column as pure horizontal lines, exactly mirroring how u and v independently control horizontal vs. vertical oscillation rate.

Deck 7, slide 16 — the formal DCT-from-DFT derivation: symmetrically extend f(x) to g(x) over 2N points, take its DFT, then t_C = Re{s∘h∘t₁}.

Why the DCT beats the DFT for compressing natural images

The counterintuitive part This directly resolves something Section 1.5 only flagged in passing: the faint bright cross artifact in sample.png's own FFT spectrum, caused by the image's own edge not matching up with itself when tiled infinitely. The DFT implicitly assumes the N-sample signal repeats periodically forever — and if f(N−1) and f(0) are very different values (as they usually are for a real photograph's edge), that implicit repetition creates a sharp discontinuity at every tile boundary, which forces the DFT to spend real energy on artificial high frequencies just to represent a jump that was never really "in" the image — it's an artifact of the periodic-tiling assumption, not the image itself. The DCT is built (via a 2N-point DFT of a symmetrically-mirrored, even-extended version of the signal — g(x) = f(x) for 0≤x<N, and g(x) = f(2N−x−1) for N≤x<2N) specifically so that this mirrored extension always matches up smoothly at the boundary, with no artificial jump. Removing that spurious discontinuity is exactly why the DCT achieves better energy compaction than the DFT on real images — it isn't wasting coefficients compensating for a jump the DFT invented.

The visual proof: a normal photograph's DCT coefficient map is almost entirely black except in the extreme top-left corner — energy overwhelmingly concentrated in the very lowest frequencies. This single image is the entire justification for why every practical image and video compression standard (JPEG, MPEG, H.26x) builds on the DCT rather than the plain DFT.

7.3 · The Discrete Sine Transform (DST), and comparing the family

Deck 7, slide 21 — the formal DST-from-DFT derivation: odd-symmetric extension g(x), 2(N+1)-point DFT, t_S = −Imag{t₁}.

The DST is built the same way as the DCT — real, orthogonal, symmetric, same frequency range as the DFT at twice the resolution — but using an odd-symmetric mirrored extension (built from a 2(N+1)-point DFT) instead of the DCT's even-symmetric one. The direct consequence: the DST has no DC component (a pure sine expansion cannot represent a nonzero constant term) and, unlike the DCT, its mirrored extension does not avoid a boundary discontinuity — so it doesn't get the DCT's energy-compaction advantage. This is exactly why the DST is a much less common choice for image compression than the DCT, even though the two formulas look almost identical.

Filter banks: the engineering bridge to wavelets

A 2D channel bank (filter bank) reframes Section 7.1's row-then-column averaging/differencing as digital signal processing: run a high-pass filter h = [1,−1] and a low-pass filter g = [1,1] along the rows, downsample by 2, then run both filters again along the columns, downsampling again. Four output combinations result — HPHP, HPLP, LPHP, LPLP — and the diagram's own labels confirm exactly the quadrant reading from Section 7.1's worked example: HPHP picks out diagonal detail, HPLP vertical detail, LPHP horizontal detail, and LPLP is the smoothed low-resolution approximation. This filter-bank machinery — plain digital filtering plus downsampling, no continuous-calculus wavelet theory required — is the actual engineering implementation behind Section 7.4's Discrete Wavelet Transform.

7.4 · The Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)

Deck 7, slide 26 — the recursive LPLP/HPLP/LPHP/HPHP pyramid sketch: each DWT pass quarters the LPLP quadrant again.
ψa,b(t) = (1/√a)·ψ((t−b)/a)

Every wavelet basis function is generated from one "mother wavelet" ψ by two operations: dilation (the scale parameter a stretches or squeezes it) and shifting (b slides it left or right). This is a fundamentally different construction from every transform so far: the DFT/DCT/DST all use one fixed-shape basis function (a sinusoid) tested at many frequencies, but a wavelet basis re-uses one fixed shape at many different scales and positions.

Filling a gap the slide leaves implicit Why does this matter enough to justify a whole new transform family? Section 1.4's counterintuitive point was that every single DFT coefficient depends on every pixel of the whole image — frequency information is global, with zero spatial locality. A wavelet coefficient, by contrast, is built from a basis function that dilation-and-shifting has confined to one specific region and one specific scale — so a wavelet transform tells you not just "how much high-frequency content is present" but "where." This is precisely the trade-off flagged back in Section 1.6's sampling-vs-frequency-resolution discussion: wavelets deliberately trade some pure frequency resolution for spatial (or temporal) locality, which the Fourier family cannot offer at all.

2D wavelets: three flavors of "detail"

A 2D wavelet transform needs one 2D scaling function φ(x,y) (the smoothing/averaging half) and three 2D wavelets, each built as a product of a 1D wavelet ψ and the 1D scaling function φ along the two axes:

  • ψH(x,y) = ψ(x)φ(y) — responds to intensity changes along columns, i.e. horizontal edges.
  • ψV(x,y) = φ(x)ψ(y) — responds to changes along rows, i.e. vertical edges.
  • ψD(x,y) = ψ(x)ψ(y) — responds to diagonal structure.

This is the exact same three-detail-plus-one-smooth structure already seen twice now — once in Section 7.1's Haar quadrants, once in Section 7.3's filter-bank labels — finally given its formal, general definition. The slide is careful to note this directional sensitivity is a "natural consequence of separability" and costs nothing extra computationally.

The analysis (decomposition) filter bank

Mechanically, this is Section 7.3's filter bank, spelled out precisely: convolve the rows with a low-pass filter hφ and a high-pass filter hψ, downsample by 2 along columns, then repeat both filters along the rows of each result and downsample again — four quarter-size outputs: Tφ (smooth approximation), TψH, TψV, TψD. Re-running this same block on Tφ alone is exactly Section 6's "idea is to expand on the LP-LP part" recursive pyramid, producing progressively coarser and coarser approximations, each with its own three detail bands at that scale — precisely the multiresolution structure the wavelet transform is named for.

Applied to a real photograph, the four quarter-size outputs read exactly as Section 7.1 predicted: the smoothed low-pass/low-pass quadrant still looks like a small, slightly blurred version of the house; the two cross-filtered quadrants pick out mostly edges (looking almost like line drawings); and the high-pass/high-pass quadrant is nearly empty, since strong diagonal-only structure is comparatively rare in most photographs.

The synthesis (reconstruction) filter bank

Perfect reconstruction runs the whole process in reverse: upsample each of the four sub-images, convolve with the matching synthesis filters, and add the results back together — recovering the next-finer scale's approximation exactly. Because this operation is invertible, no information from the original image is lost by decomposing it — only reorganized into a smooth-approximation-plus-detail form, which is precisely why wavelet-based coding (used in JPEG-2000) can compress by simply discarding the least visually important detail coefficients before running this same reconstruction.

DWT vs. DCT: an honest trade-off

The deck's own summary is worth reproducing directly, because it is exactly the kind of "argued point, not settled fact" the source material presents as a straightforward pro/con list:

DWT advantages over DCTDWT disadvantages
Higher compression ratio; avoids blocking artifactsHigher computational cost
Good localization in both space and scaleLarger basis functions can introduce blurring and ringing
Better match to human perceptual relevanceLower quality than JPEG at low compression rates in some cases

No single transform in this section is a strict improvement on the others — Haar is fastest but compacts energy poorly; DCT compacts energy excellently and avoids DWT's blur/ringing risk but produces blocking artifacts at high compression; DWT avoids blocking and localizes well but costs more to compute and can blur at very low bitrates. Sections 8–11 return to this trade-off directly: Section 11's JPEG standard is built on the DCT, while the deck notes JPEG-2000 (its wavelet-based successor) is built on the DWT.
