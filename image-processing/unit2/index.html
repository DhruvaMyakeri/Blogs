Unit 2 Study Guide

This unit takes an image out of the pixel grid entirely — into the frequency domain, then into a family of increasingly clever transform bases — and ends by asking the practical question every one of those detours was really in service of: how small can an image file actually get? Eleven lecture decks, restructured here into eleven concept-driven sections, with every filter, transform, and code demonstrated live on one working photograph (sample.png) so the numbers in the text are numbers you can see.

The arc of the unit

Three big movements, each building directly on the last:

I. Frequency domain fundamentals (Sections 1–5)

What "frequency" means for an image, why the Fourier transform lets you filter by simple multiplication instead of sliding-window convolution, what can go wrong when you sample a continuous scene onto a discrete grid, and the three canonical things you can do to a spectrum once you're looking at one: smooth it, sharpen it, or fix its lighting.

II. Beyond Fourier — choosing a better basis (Sections 6–7)

Every transform in Part I turns out to be one special case of a much more general idea: re-describe a signal against any orthonormal set of reference patterns, not just sinusoids. Once that's explicit, new choices of basis — Haar's local plus/minus pulses, the DCT's boundary-friendly cosines, wavelets' zoom-in-and-out pyramids — become available, each trading off energy compaction, computational cost, and spatial locality differently.

III. Compression — putting the energy-compaction idea to work (Sections 8–11)

A transform that concentrates most of an image's energy into a handful of coefficients is most of the way to a compression scheme already. The last four sections close the loop: measuring redundancy, building an optimal entropy code (Huffman), exploiting spatial repetition directly (run-length, symbol-based, bit-plane coding), and finally assembling all of it — block-wise DCT, quantization, zigzag ordering, run-length coding, Huffman coding — into the JPEG standard itself.

Sections

Section 1

Frequency Domain & the Fourier Transform

What spatial frequency is, the Fourier series/transform, the DFT, and why filtering in frequency space is so much cheaper than spatial convolution.
Section 2

Sampling, Aliasing & Interpolation

The sampling theorem, why aliasing can look perfectly normal and be completely wrong, and how resizing an image is itself a sampling problem.
Section 3

Smoothing Filters in the Frequency Domain

Ideal, Gaussian, and Butterworth low-pass filters — and the ringing artifact that separates them.
Section 4

Sharpening Filters in the Frequency Domain

High-pass filtering as the mirror image of Section 3, and why a pure high-pass result looks like line art.
Section 5

Unsharp Masking & Homomorphic Filtering

Adding high-frequency content back instead of discarding everything else, and separating illumination from reflectance with a logarithm.
Section 6

Matrix-Based Transforms & Basis Images

Every transform as one matrix multiply — orthonormal bases, energy conservation, compaction, and decorrelation.
Section 7

Haar, DCT, DST and Wavelet Transforms

A local, blocky basis; a boundary-friendly cosine basis; and a multiresolution wavelet basis — three genuinely different answers to "what should the reference patterns be?"
Section 8

Image Compression Fundamentals

Coding, spatial, and psychovisual redundancy; compression ratio and fidelity; the encoder/decoder pipeline every scheme in this unit fits into.
Section 9

Huffman Coding

Entropy as a theoretical floor, the Huffman algorithm, and a live in-browser demo building a real Huffman code from this guide's own working image.
Section 10

Run-Length, Symbol-Based & Bit-Plane Coding

RLE and the CCITT fax standards, symbol dictionaries and JBIG2, and slicing an image into its 8 constituent bit-planes.
Section 11

Block Transform Coding & JPEG

Where every earlier idea converges: 8×8 DCT blocks, quantization, zigzag ordering, run-length + Huffman coding — and the wavelet-based JPEG-2000 that followed it.

How this guide is built

Every lecture slide from the original eleven decks appears somewhere in these eleven sections — reordered by concept rather than by original slide order where that made the material clearer (most visibly, Deck 2's sampling/aliasing content is separated from its Fourier-transform recap, which is folded into Section 1 instead). Slides that are pure recaps of material already explained in full are shown as a screenshot with a short caption rather than re-explained from scratch.

Wherever a filter, transform, or code makes more sense seen than described, this guide runs it for real on a single working photograph and shows the actual output — not a stock textbook figure. Every number quoted in a simulation box (entropy, compression ratios, energy percentages) was computed directly from that image, not invented for the example.
